Movement & Strength

Do 5-minute workouts actually make a difference?

If five minutes is all you have, is it even worth bothering? Research suggests short bursts of activity can be useful — especially when they're repeated throughout your day or week.

LS Companion · 6 min read

A five-minute workout isn't a replacement for every other kind of exercise. But research increasingly suggests that short bouts of activity can contribute to your fitness and overall physical activity.

And that matters if finding a spare 30 or 60 minutes to exercise feels almost impossible.

Exercise doesn't have to happen all at once

We often think about exercise as a formal workout: change your clothes, go to the gym, exercise for 30–60 minutes, shower, and get on with your day.

But your body doesn't only benefit from movement when it happens in a long, structured session.

Current physical activity guidelines emphasize accumulating physical activity and reducing sedentary time. Researchers have also been studying something sometimes called “exercise snacks” — very short bouts of purposeful activity scattered throughout the day.

That might look like:

  • climbing the stairs quickly
  • doing squats beside your desk
  • taking a brisk walk around the block
  • doing a few minutes of bodyweight exercises
  • getting your heart rate up for a few minutes before returning to your day

What happens when people actually do this?

Researchers have tested short bouts of exercise in people who are otherwise relatively inactive. Studies vary in exactly how the exercise is performed, but the overall idea is similar: brief periods of activity are repeated across the day rather than completed as one traditional workout.

Reviews of this research suggest that this approach can improve cardiorespiratory fitness, although the evidence for other outcomes — such as body composition, blood pressure and blood lipids — is less consistent.

Another potentially important advantage is practical rather than physiological: short bouts of exercise may simply be easier to fit into real life.

Even tiny bursts of vigorous movement may matter

Researchers have also studied vigorous activity that happens during ordinary life rather than during a formal workout.

This has been described as vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity (VILPA) — things like walking very quickly uphill, climbing stairs or rushing somewhere for a minute or two.

Large observational studies have found associations between small amounts of this kind of vigorous everyday movement and lower risks of cardiovascular disease and premature death.

That's encouraging, but it's important to understand what that means: observational research can show an association, but it can't prove that a few minutes of vigorous activity caused the lower risk.

Five minutes isn't magic

There isn't a special biological switch that flips at five minutes.

And doing five minutes of exercise doesn't mean you've suddenly met all of your body's needs for physical activity.

The World Health Organization recommends that adults aim for 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, along with muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days a week.

Short bouts are better thought of as another way of accumulating movement.

Five minutes before work. Three minutes while dinner cooks. A brisk walk after lunch. Ten squats while you're waiting for the kettle. Another five minutes later.

Suddenly, “I don't have time to exercise” doesn't necessarily have to mean “I can't do anything today.”

The takeaway: short bouts of exercise count. When the alternative is doing nothing because you don't have time for a “proper workout,” five minutes can be a very worthwhile place to start.

And the evidence isn't perfect

Research into very short bouts of exercise is still developing. Studies use different exercises, intensities and schedules, and many involve relatively small groups of participants.

Researchers also haven't found that tiny workouts improve every health measure they have studied.

So claiming that five-minute workouts can accomplish everything a traditional exercise program can would be getting ahead of the science.

The more useful message is simpler: doing something can be worthwhile even when you don't have time to do everything.

Try this: a five-minute movement break

You don't need equipment. Here's one simple way to break up five minutes:

1 minute: brisk walking or marching
1 minute: sit-to-stands from a chair
1 minute: wall push-ups
1 minute: calf raises
1 minute: brisk walking or marching

Or forget the timer entirely. Pick something that gets you moving and do it for a few minutes.

Taking care of yourself doesn't always have to mean finding another hour in your day.

Sometimes it can mean making better use of five minutes you already have.
