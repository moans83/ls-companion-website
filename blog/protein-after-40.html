Do women actually need more protein after 40?

Protein has become one of the biggest nutrition messages aimed at women in midlife. Some of the attention is justified — but turning 40 doesn't suddenly create a new protein requirement.

LS Companion · 7 min read

If you're a woman in your 40s, you've probably encountered some version of the message: you need to eat more protein now.

Sometimes that advice comes with very specific targets, claims about perimenopause, or the suggestion that every meal needs a large dose of protein.

There's a sensible idea underneath all of this. Protein provides the amino acids our bodies use to build and maintain muscle, and preserving muscle becomes increasingly important as we get older.

But there isn't a biological switch that flips on your 40th birthday.

So why is everyone suddenly talking about protein?

Part of the reason is muscle.

Muscle mass and strength tend to decline with aging, although the rate varies considerably between people and is influenced by physical activity, health, nutrition and other factors.

Older muscle can also become less responsive to the muscle-building stimulus from a given amount of dietary protein — sometimes described as anabolic resistance.

That's one reason expert groups focused on older adults have proposed protein intakes somewhat above the standard adult recommendation.

But here's the part that's often lost online: those recommendations generally refer to older adults, often people over 65 — not specifically every woman once she reaches 40.

Forty isn't a nutritional cliff. Protein matters throughout adulthood, and preserving muscle becomes increasingly important with age — but there isn't a special protein requirement that suddenly begins on your 40th birthday.

What about perimenopause and menopause?

This is where the conversation gets more complicated.

The menopause transition coincides with changes in body composition and musculoskeletal health, and researchers are actively studying how exercise and nutrition might help.

But evidence specifically establishing an ideal protein target for women during perimenopause is limited.

In fact, much of the research used to support higher protein recommendations comes from studies of older adults generally, athletes, or people undertaking resistance training — rather than trials designed specifically around women in their 40s going through perimenopause.

That doesn't mean protein isn't important during this stage. It means we should be careful about presenting a precise “perimenopause protein prescription” as settled science.

Protein becomes particularly useful when you strength train

This is one area where the evidence is considerably stronger.

Resistance training provides a powerful stimulus for maintaining and building muscle. Consuming adequate protein provides the amino acids needed to support that adaptation.

A large meta-analysis of randomized trials found that adding protein supplementation to prolonged resistance training produced modest additional improvements in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults.

Importantly, the benefits didn't keep increasing indefinitely as protein intake went higher.

In that analysis, increases in total protein intake beyond about 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day did not produce further gains in fat-free mass during resistance training.

That's not a recommendation that everyone should eat 1.6 g/kg. It's evidence against the idea that continually increasing protein necessarily produces continually greater muscle gains.

How much protein do you actually need?

There isn't one ideal number for every woman.

Protein needs depend on factors including body size, age, activity, overall diet, health and training goals.

The standard adult protein recommendation is commonly expressed as approximately 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. That number is intended to cover the basic needs of most healthy adults; it isn't necessarily an “optimal muscle-building target.”

For healthy adults over about 65, expert groups such as PROT-AGE and ESPEN have recommended around 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day to help maintain muscle and function, with somewhat higher intakes sometimes suggested for active older adults or during illness.

Again, those are recommendations for older adults. We shouldn't quietly turn them into an official recommendation for healthy 40-year-old women.

What do those numbers look like?

For context only, consider someone who weighs 70 kg (about 154 lb).

  • 0.8 g/kg = about 56 g of protein per day
  • 1.0 g/kg = about 70 g per day
  • 1.2 g/kg = about 84 g per day
  • 1.6 g/kg = about 112 g per day

These examples aren't a prescription. They simply show how dramatically different protein advice can sound depending on which target someone chooses.

Do you need protein powder?

No.

Protein supplements can be convenient, especially if you're finding it difficult to get enough protein from your usual meals or you're training regularly.

But there's nothing uniquely necessary about getting protein from a powder.

Protein can come from foods such as dairy products, eggs, fish, poultry, meat, soy foods, beans, lentils, peas, nuts and seeds. Different foods also bring different nutrients with them, which is one reason it's worth thinking about your overall diet rather than protein grams alone.

Simple ways to include protein

You don't need to rebuild your entire diet around protein. Look at meals you already eat and ask whether there's a useful protein source there.

Breakfast: eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese or a soy-based option.
Lunch: beans, lentils, tofu, fish, chicken or another protein-rich food added to a salad, bowl or sandwich.
Snack: yogurt, nuts, edamame or another convenient protein-containing food.
Dinner: build the meal around a protein source you enjoy alongside vegetables, whole grains or other foods.

A protein shake can also be perfectly reasonable if convenience is the thing that helps you meet your needs. It just isn't mandatory.

Does protein timing matter?

You may also have heard that protein has to be consumed within a very narrow window after exercise.

Timing can influence muscle protein synthesis, and research continues to examine whether distributing protein across meals or consuming it around exercise provides additional advantages.

But for most people, getting enough protein overall and actually doing resistance training are bigger priorities than perfectly timing a shake.

There's no need to panic because you didn't drink protein within 30 minutes of putting down a dumbbell.

Protein isn't the whole muscle story

This may be the most important point.

Eating more protein by itself isn't the same thing as giving your muscles a reason to stay strong.

Resistance training is a direct stimulus for muscular adaptation. Protein supports that process.

Sleep, sufficient overall nutrition, physical activity and health also matter.

So if the choice is between obsessing over whether breakfast contained exactly 30 grams of protein and beginning some form of regular resistance exercise, the exercise deserves a prominent place in the conversation.

Protein matters. But you don't need to treat it like a new medication you were prescribed at 40. Eat enough, include protein-rich foods you enjoy, and pair good nutrition with something that actually challenges your muscles.
