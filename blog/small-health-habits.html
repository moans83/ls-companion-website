Too busy for a full workout? Small amounts of exercise still count

When life gets busy, exercise can easily become all-or-nothing: either you have time for a proper workout or you do nothing. Fortunately, your body doesn't work that way.

LS Companion · 7 min read

Some weeks, finding an hour to exercise is realistic.

Other weeks, work, commuting, children, appointments and everything else seem to consume every available minute.

That's when exercise can become strangely binary. If you can't fit in the workout you think you should be doing, it can feel as though there's little point doing anything.

But exercise research gives us a more useful way to think about it.

More exercise can produce greater benefits, but the smallest useful amount isn't necessarily a full workout.

There is a difference between optimal and worthwhile

This distinction matters.

If your goal is to maximize strength, endurance or muscle growth, you'll generally need enough training volume and intensity to support that goal.

Ten casual squats while you're waiting for the kettle shouldn't be presented as equivalent to a well-designed resistance-training program.

But “not optimal” and “not useful” are very different things.

Researchers have become increasingly interested in minimal-dose exercise: how much benefit can be achieved when the amount of exercise is deliberately kept small?

Surprisingly little resistance training can improve strength

A 2024 review examined several minimal-dose resistance-training approaches, including single-set training, one weekly session and brief “resistance exercise snacks.”

The evidence was strongest for weekly and single-set approaches, while resistance exercise snacking was described as promising but still emerging.

Importantly, the review concluded that small volumes of resistance exercise can increase strength — particularly when compared with doing no resistance exercise at all.

That doesn't establish a magic minimum. The effectiveness of a small amount of exercise still depends on factors such as the exercise, resistance, effort, your current fitness and how consistently you do it.

Maintaining fitness may take less than building it

There's another idea that can be especially useful during a busy period: your goal doesn't always have to be improvement.

Sometimes maintaining what you've already built is a perfectly reasonable goal.

A review looking specifically at reduced training found that physical performance can sometimes be maintained despite substantial reductions in training volume or frequency.

In younger populations, strength and muscle size were maintained in some studies with as little as one strength session per week and one set per exercise for periods of several months, provided training intensity was maintained.

Older adults may require somewhat more training to maintain muscle size, and the evidence isn't strong enough to turn these findings into a universal prescription.

But there is an important practical lesson: maintaining your baseline may require less exercise than you imagine.

A busy week doesn't have to be a lost week. You may not be maximizing your fitness, but small, sufficiently challenging bouts of activity can still be worthwhile.

What does “keep your baseline” actually mean?

It doesn't mean there's a scientifically defined baseline that you can preserve by doing a prescribed number of squats each morning.

Think of it instead as a practical mindset.

During a week when formal workouts aren't happening, you still look for opportunities to use your body.

You might do some push-ups one evening. Squats while dinner cooks. Take the stairs at work. Walk briskly for ten minutes. Pick up your dumbbells for a few sets while you're waiting for something else.

Some of those activities may provide a meaningful training stimulus. Others may simply add movement to an otherwise sedentary day.

Both can be worthwhile — without pretending they're the same thing.

What if you finally have time on the weekend?

Take advantage of it.

There's no rule that says exercise has to be distributed evenly across seven days.

Observational research on so-called “weekend warriors” has found that people who achieve recommended amounts of moderate-to-vigorous activity in only one or two sessions can still have substantially better health outcomes than inactive people.

In a large U.S. cohort study, people who met recommended activity levels in one or two weekly sessions did not have significantly different mortality rates from people who spread their activity over three or more sessions.

That doesn't prove that every training goal is equally well served by one weekend workout. It also doesn't mean you should suddenly cram a week's worth of intense exercise into Saturday if your body isn't prepared for it.

But if Saturday is when you have time for a run, hike, bike ride or longer workout, it absolutely doesn't become worthless because you couldn't exercise for an hour on Tuesday.

There's also the question of habit

There may be a psychological advantage to keeping some movement in your week too.

Research finds a relationship between stronger physical-activity habits and physical activity itself, and interventions designed to build physical-activity habits can improve measures of habit strength.

But we should be careful about taking that evidence too far.

We can't say that doing ten squats on Wednesday will make you more likely to go running on Saturday.

What we can say is that making movement a regular part of everyday situations is consistent with what researchers know about habit formation.

And if small amounts of activity help you avoid the feeling that you've completely fallen out of your routine, that can be a useful strategy in its own right.

Think in opportunities, not workouts

On days when a formal workout isn't happening, look for realistic opportunities to move or challenge your muscles.

Making coffee: squats or calf raises while you wait.
A few spare minutes: a set or two of push-ups, rows or another resistance exercise.
At work: walk somewhere instead of automatically staying at your desk.
After a meal: take a short walk.
A free hour appears: use it for the longer run, workout, hike or activity you haven't been able to fit in.

The goal isn't to convince yourself that five minutes and fifty minutes are identical. It's to stop treating fifty minutes as the price of admission.

Small doesn't have to mean easy

There's one final caveat, particularly for strength.

If you want a movement to maintain or improve muscular strength, your muscles generally need to be meaningfully challenged.

As you get stronger, the same ten easy squats may no longer provide much resistance. You might eventually need more repetitions, a harder variation, added resistance or a different exercise.

This is why the message isn't simply “do anything for two minutes and you'll stay fit.”

It's more useful than that:

Do what fits today. Make some of it challenging. Take the bigger opportunities when you have them. And don't let an imperfect week convince you that your only option is to start over next week.
