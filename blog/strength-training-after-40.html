Why strength training matters more as we get older

Strength training isn't just about lifting heavier weights or changing how your body looks. As we get older, maintaining muscle and strength becomes increasingly important for how well our bodies function.

LS Companion · 7 min read

If you're in your 40s, you may have started hearing a lot more about strength training.

Some of that attention is justified. Muscle mass and strength don't remain constant throughout adulthood, and declines become more important as we get older.

But this doesn't mean that turning 40 suddenly changes your muscles, or that every woman needs an elaborate weightlifting program.

The useful message is much simpler: muscle and strength are worth deliberately maintaining, and resistance training is one of the most effective ways to do that.

What actually happens to muscle as we age?

Adults tend to lose muscle mass and strength as they get older. The process isn't identical for everyone, and age isn't the only factor involved.

Physical activity, illness, nutrition and other factors all influence how much muscle and strength someone maintains. Strength can also decline differently from muscle mass itself.

That's one reason it can be misleading to frame this as something that suddenly begins at a particular birthday.

What matters practically is that maintaining strength becomes increasingly valuable over the decades ahead.

Why does strength matter?

We use muscular strength constantly, even when we aren't thinking about it.

Getting out of a chair. Carrying groceries. Lifting something from the floor. Climbing stairs. Picking up a child. Getting luggage into an overhead compartment.

Research in older adults consistently shows that resistance training can improve muscular strength and physical function. Studies in postmenopausal women have also found improvements in upper- and lower-body strength following resistance training.

That doesn't mean strength training guarantees independence or prevents every age-related physical problem. But strength is an important component of our ability to perform everyday physical tasks.

What about menopause?

Menopause is often mentioned in conversations about muscle because the hormonal changes that occur across the menopause transition coincide with changes in body composition, muscle and bone.

Separating the effects of menopause itself from chronological aging, activity levels and other factors isn't always simple. So claims that menopause suddenly causes dramatic muscle loss can overstate what the evidence can tell us.

What we do know is that resistance training remains effective after menopause.

Systematic reviews of randomized trials in postmenopausal women have found that resistance training improves muscular strength and aspects of physical fitness.

Strength training may also help your bones

Bone health becomes increasingly important for women as they age, particularly after menopause.

Exercise places mechanical loads on bone, which is one reason resistance and weight-bearing exercise are studied as ways to help maintain bone strength.

Meta-analyses in postmenopausal women generally suggest that exercise — including resistance training — can have beneficial effects on bone mineral density at sites such as the lumbar spine, femoral neck and hip.

But the details matter. Studies differ substantially in the exercises used, intensity, frequency and length of training. Researchers are still working out which programs are most effective.

So it's reasonable to say resistance training can support bone health. It's much harder to claim that a particular simple routine will prevent osteoporosis.

Strength training isn't valuable because women suddenly become “old” at 40. It's valuable because muscle and strength are useful throughout life — and become increasingly worth protecting as the decades go by.

Does strength training mean lifting heavy weights?

Not necessarily.

Resistance training simply means your muscles are working against resistance. That resistance can come from dumbbells, barbells, resistance bands, machines or your own body weight.

The resistance does need to challenge your muscles if you want them to adapt. Doing an exercise that feels extremely easy indefinitely is unlikely to produce the same gains as gradually increasing the challenge.

But “challenging” is relative. Someone beginning strength training and someone who has trained for ten years shouldn't be using the same weights or exercises.

How much do you actually need?

You don't need to strength train every day.

Public-health guidelines generally recommend that adults perform muscle-strengthening activities involving the major muscle groups on two or more days each week.

Research programs often use more structured sessions than that, and the ideal program depends on what you're trying to achieve. Training specifically to maximize muscle growth or strength is different from simply trying to maintain useful strength and physical function.

The encouraging part is that starting doesn't require a complicated program.

A simple place to start

A basic routine can focus on movements that use the major muscle groups. For example:

Squat: sit-to-stands or squats
Push: wall, counter or floor push-ups
Pull: resistance-band or dumbbell rows
Hinge: a hip-hinge or deadlift movement
Carry: carrying dumbbells or another appropriate load

These aren't magic exercises, and they're not appropriate for everyone. They're simply examples of common movement patterns that can be trained in many different ways.

You don't have to become a “weightlifter”

One reason strength training can feel intimidating is that we often picture a gym, a rack of heavy weights and an hour-long workout.

That's one way to strength train. It isn't the only way.

A short session at home counts. Resistance bands count. Dumbbells beside your desk count. Bodyweight exercises can count.

If you enjoy going to a gym and lifting heavy weights, great. If you don't, you can still find ways to challenge your muscles.

The goal isn't to train a particular way because you've reached a certain age.

It's to keep giving your body reasons to stay strong.
