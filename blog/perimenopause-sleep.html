Perimenopause & Menopause

Why can sleep get so difficult during perimenopause?

If your sleep seems to have changed in your 40s, you're not imagining that this can happen. But perimenopausal sleep problems aren't caused by one hormone — or one single thing.

LS Companion · 7 min read

Maybe you used to sleep through the night and now you're awake at 3 a.m.

Maybe falling asleep has become harder. Or you're waking hot, sweaty or inexplicably alert and then struggling to fall asleep again.

Sleep complaints become more common during the menopausal transition. But the explanation isn't as simple as “your estrogen is dropping.”

Sleep during midlife can be affected by hormonal changes, hot flashes and night sweats, mood, stress, aging, health conditions and sleep disorders — often with several things happening at once.

First: sleep problems really are common during this transition

Research consistently finds a greater burden of sleep problems among women during perimenopause and after menopause compared with premenopausal women.

But estimates vary considerably depending on what researchers mean by a “sleep problem,” how they measure it and which women they study.

That's important because poor sleep can mean very different things: trouble falling asleep, waking repeatedly, waking too early, feeling unrefreshed despite sleeping, or having an underlying sleep disorder.

In other words, “menopause sleep” isn't one condition.

Are hormones responsible?

Reproductive hormones change substantially across the menopausal transition, and there is evidence linking these changes with sleep.

But researchers haven't found a simple relationship where a particular estrogen level predicts whether a woman will sleep well or badly.

That's partly because sleep itself is complicated — and because hormonal changes occur alongside changes in vasomotor symptoms, mood, aging and everyday life.

So hormones are part of the story. They aren't necessarily the whole story.

Hot flashes and night sweats can absolutely disrupt sleep

Vasomotor symptoms — hot flashes and night sweats — have one of the clearest relationships with disturbed sleep during the menopause transition.

A night sweat can wake you directly. You may then need to cool down, remove bedding or change clothes before trying to fall asleep again.

Studies using objective sleep measurements also support an association between nighttime vasomotor symptoms and awakenings.

But there's another important nuance: not every sleep problem during perimenopause is caused by hot flashes.

Some women develop insomnia without prominent vasomotor symptoms, while others continue having difficulty sleeping even when the hot flashes are better controlled.

Sometimes insomnia becomes a problem of its own

A few bad nights are not the same thing as chronic insomnia.

Insomnia disorder involves persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep or waking earlier than intended, together with daytime consequences, despite having adequate opportunity to sleep.

Once insomnia becomes established, the original trigger isn't always the only thing keeping it going.

Worrying about whether you'll sleep, spending excessive time awake in bed, changing your schedule to compensate for poor nights and other behaviours can become part of the cycle.

That's one reason simply telling someone with persistent insomnia to improve their “sleep hygiene” may not be enough.

Perimenopause can contribute to sleep disruption, but waking at night doesn't automatically mean “it's just hormones.” Figuring out what is actually disrupting your sleep matters.

What actually helps?

It depends on the cause.

For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is an evidence-based treatment and has also been studied specifically in women experiencing menopausal insomnia.

CBT-I is more than general advice about keeping your bedroom dark or avoiding your phone before bed. It uses specific behavioural and cognitive strategies to change patterns that perpetuate insomnia.

If hot flashes or night sweats are driving the awakenings, treating the vasomotor symptoms may improve sleep as well. Menopausal hormone therapy is effective for vasomotor symptoms and can improve sleep in some women, particularly when sleep is being disrupted by those symptoms.

Hormone therapy isn't appropriate or desired by everyone, and non-hormonal treatment options for vasomotor symptoms also exist. The best choice depends on symptoms, medical history, risks and personal preferences.

Don't assume every sleep problem is perimenopause

This may be the most useful thing to know.

Midlife is also a time when conditions such as obstructive sleep apnea and restless legs syndrome deserve consideration. Both can disrupt sleep and may be mistaken for ordinary insomnia.

Sleep apnea isn't limited to men, and women don't always present with the stereotypical picture of very loud snoring and obvious witnessed pauses in breathing.

Symptoms can include unrefreshing sleep, daytime tiredness, morning headaches, insomnia-like complaints and snoring.

When it's worth talking to a healthcare professional

Consider bringing up your sleep if it's persistent, affecting your daytime life or simply becoming difficult to manage — particularly if you have:

  • ongoing difficulty falling or staying asleep
  • frequent night sweats or hot flashes
  • loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing
  • significant daytime sleepiness or exhaustion
  • an uncomfortable urge to move your legs at night
  • new or worsening anxiety or low mood

The point isn't that these symptoms necessarily indicate something serious. It's that there may be something identifiable — and treatable — behind your poor sleep.

What about the usual sleep advice?

A regular sleep schedule, a comfortable bedroom, limiting caffeine later in the day and avoiding things that you know interfere with your sleep are all reasonable.

Exercise is also associated with better sleep and has many other health benefits.

But lifestyle advice shouldn't become another way of blaming women for symptoms they haven't managed to optimize away.

If you're waking repeatedly because of severe night sweats, treating the night sweats may matter more than buying blackout curtains.

If you have chronic insomnia, CBT-I is more targeted than a list of generic sleep-hygiene tips.

And if you have sleep apnea, neither of those approaches treats the underlying breathing disorder.

Poor sleep during perimenopause is real, common and often multifactorial. Rather than assuming you simply need to “sleep better,” it can be more useful to ask: what is waking me up — or keeping me awake?
