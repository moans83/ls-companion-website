Movement & Strength

Stuck at a desk all day? Small ways to move more

An office job can make it surprisingly difficult to move much during the day. The good news is that movement doesn't have to come entirely from a workout before or after work.

LS Companion · 6 min read

You arrive at work, sit down, open your laptop — and suddenly several hours have passed.

Even if you exercise regularly, an office job can involve a lot of sitting. And if you're already struggling to find time for formal exercise, advice to simply “work out more” isn't always particularly useful.

There is another piece of the picture: how much we move during the rest of the day.

Why does sitting matter?

Sedentary behaviour means spending waking time sitting, reclining or lying down while using very little energy. Desk work is a common source of it.

Large observational studies have linked greater amounts of sedentary time with poorer health outcomes, including higher risks of cardiovascular disease and premature death.

But there's an important nuance: sitting isn't a poison, and there isn't a precise number of minutes after which sitting suddenly becomes harmful.

The relationship also depends partly on how physically active someone is overall. Higher levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity appear to reduce some of the risks associated with spending large amounts of time sitting.

Can you just exercise after work and forget about sitting?

Not quite — but the story is more reassuring than the phrase “sitting is the new smoking” suggests.

Exercise matters a great deal.

In a large harmonized meta-analysis involving more than one million adults, high levels of moderate-intensity physical activity appeared to eliminate the increased mortality risk associated with long periods of sitting.

That doesn't mean sitting all day is ideal. It does mean that we shouldn't talk about sitting as though its effects are completely independent of the rest of your physical activity.

What about getting up every so often?

Researchers have also tested what happens when long periods of sitting are interrupted with short activity breaks.

Controlled studies suggest that breaking up prolonged sitting with brief periods of activity can improve some short-term metabolic responses, particularly blood glucose and insulin after meals.

The size of the effect varies between studies, and this research doesn't prove that taking a two-minute walk every half hour will prevent diabetes or heart disease.

But it gives us a reasonable basis for a very practical idea: when you can, break up long periods of sitting with movement.

You don't need to turn your office into a gym. Think of movement during the workday as an addition to regular exercise: opportunities to sit a little less and move a little more.

The easiest movement may be the movement already built into your day

One challenge with health advice is that it often gives us another task to find time for.

Instead, look for places where movement can attach itself to something you're already doing.

A phone call can become a walking call. Going for coffee can include taking the longer route. A trip to another floor can involve the stairs. Lunch can include a short walk.

None of these things individually represents a complete exercise program.

They simply increase the amount of movement in a day that might otherwise involve many consecutive hours in a chair.

Can you actually do strength exercises at work?

Yes — although it's worth being clear about what they can and can't accomplish.

Sit-to-stands from a chair, calf raises, wall or counter push-ups and other simple movements can all make your muscles work.

Whether they provide enough resistance to substantially increase your strength depends on your current ability and how challenging the exercise is.

Ten easy squats beside your desk aren't automatically equivalent to a progressive resistance-training program.

But that doesn't make them pointless. They can add movement, interrupt sitting and, if an exercise is sufficiently challenging, contribute to muscular activity.

Realistic ways to move more at work

You don't need to do all of these. Pick the ones that actually fit your workplace and your day.

Waiting for your coffee: try some calf raises or sit-to-stands.
On a call: stand up or walk if you don't need to be at your screen.
Going between floors: take the stairs when it's practical for you.
After lunch: take a short walk, even if you only have a few minutes.
Between tasks: stand up, walk to get water or take a quick lap around the office.
At your desk: keep a resistance band or light dumbbells nearby if your workplace allows it and you'll actually use them.

Do you need a standing desk?

A standing desk can reduce sitting time, and some people simply prefer having the option to change positions.

But standing still isn't the same thing as physical activity. It uses somewhat more energy than sitting, but the difference is relatively small.

So a standing desk can be one tool for changing how you spend your workday, but it shouldn't be treated as a substitute for walking, aerobic activity or strength training.

Small movement doesn't replace exercise

This is where it's easy for the message to become more exciting than the evidence.

Taking the stairs, walking during a call and doing calf raises while waiting for the printer are useful ways to move more. They don't make broader physical activity recommendations irrelevant.

The World Health Organization recommends that adults aim for 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, as well as muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days per week.

But guidelines describe a target. They don't mean movement below that target has no value.

If your workday currently involves eight hours of almost uninterrupted sitting, finding several opportunities to move is a meaningful change — even if you're not suddenly becoming the most active person in the office.

Don't wait for the perfect 60-minute workout before movement “counts.” Move when you can, break up long stretches of sitting, and keep working toward regular aerobic and strength activity too.
