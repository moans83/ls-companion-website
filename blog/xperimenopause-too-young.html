Perimenopause & Menopause

Can you be too young for perimenopause?

If you've brought up changing periods, hot flashes or other symptoms and been told you're “too young” for perimenopause, age is relevant — but it isn't the whole answer.

LS Companion · 7 min read

“You're too young for perimenopause.”

It's a frustrating answer if your body has changed and you're trying to understand why.

But there's an important middle ground between assuming every symptom in your 30s or early 40s is perimenopause and dismissing the possibility entirely because of your age.

Age changes how likely perimenopause is and how it should be evaluated. It doesn't mean symptoms should simply be ignored.

So, can you actually be “too young” for perimenopause?

Natural menopause — the point when 12 months have passed without a menstrual period — occurs at an average age of around 51. Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause and can last for several years.

That means someone in their late 30s is less likely to be going through the usual menopausal transition than someone in their mid-to-late 40s.

But less likely doesn't mean impossible, and current clinical guidelines specifically address younger women with possible menopausal symptoms.

The 2025 European Society of Endocrinology guideline recommends considering perimenopause in women aged 40 to under 45 when menstrual irregularity and/or vasomotor symptoms such as hot flashes or sweats are present.

NICE guidance similarly recognizes early menopause between ages 40 and 44 and says FSH testing can be considered in people aged 40 to 45 who have menopause-associated symptoms and a change in their menstrual cycle.

Being 40, 41, 42 or 43 is not, by itself, a reason to rule out perimenopause.

What if you're under 40?

Under 40, the clinical question becomes somewhat different.

Menopause-like symptoms or significant menstrual changes at this age shouldn't simply be labelled routine perimenopause. One important possibility clinicians need to consider is premature ovarian insufficiency (POI).

POI occurs when ovarian function is lost or substantially impaired before age 40. It can involve irregular or absent periods, difficulty conceiving and symptoms associated with low oestrogen, including hot flashes.

This distinction matters because POI isn't just a name for “really early perimenopause.” It has implications for fertility, bone health and longer-term health, and appropriate evaluation and treatment are important.

The European Society of Endocrinology recommends considering POI in women under 40 who have menstrual irregularity, unexplained subfertility and/or vasomotor symptoms.

Do your periods have to become irregular first?

Menstrual changes are one of the most useful clues when clinicians are evaluating the menopausal transition.

Cycles may become shorter, longer or less predictable, and eventually periods may be skipped.

But the overall clinical picture matters. Some symptoms commonly discussed in relation to perimenopause — such as poor sleep, low mood, anxiety, headaches or difficulty concentrating — can also occur for many other reasons.

Hot flashes and night sweats combined with changing menstrual patterns provide a more characteristic picture than a symptom such as fatigue on its own.

What if your hormone test was “normal”?

This is where perimenopause can become particularly confusing.

Follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH, changes during the menopausal transition — but it can also fluctuate considerably.

The European Society of Endocrinology notes that a normal FSH level does not rule out perimenopause. That's one reason routine hormone testing isn't required to diagnose perimenopause in an otherwise healthy person over 45 with a typical presentation.

Age matters here too.

NICE says FSH testing can be considered in people aged 40 to 45 who have menopause-associated symptoms, including menstrual changes, and in people under 40 when menopause is suspected.

The European guideline similarly says biochemical testing can be considered between 40 and 45, while women under 40 with suggestive symptoms should be evaluated for POI.

A single “normal” hormone result doesn't necessarily answer the question of perimenopause. But symptoms alone shouldn't be used to assume perimenopause either.

What else could cause similar symptoms?

Being taken seriously doesn't necessarily mean your clinician should conclude that you are in perimenopause.

A good evaluation also considers other explanations.

Menstrual changes and menopause-like symptoms can overlap with other conditions. Depending on your symptoms and circumstances, clinicians may consider possibilities such as pregnancy, thyroid disorders, high prolactin levels, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) or functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea.

Medications and hormonal contraception can also change bleeding patterns or make menopausal status harder to determine.

So if a clinician doesn't think your symptoms are caused by perimenopause, a reasonable next question is: what do they think could be causing them instead?

What should you track before your appointment?

A vague feeling that “something has changed” can be difficult to explain during a short appointment. A simple record can make the conversation much more concrete.

It may help to bring:

  • your age and approximately when your symptoms started
  • what your menstrual cycles used to look like compared with now
  • changes in cycle length, skipped periods or bleeding
  • hot flashes or night sweats
  • changes in sleep
  • vaginal, vulvar or urinary symptoms
  • mood or cognitive changes you've noticed
  • medications, supplements and hormonal contraception you use
  • relevant medical and reproductive history
  • family history, including your mother's age at menopause if known
  • how much the symptoms are affecting your sleep, work or daily life

These aren't just things to collect for the sake of tracking. Menstrual patterns, menopausal symptoms, medications, personal medical history and family history are all part of the clinical assessment described in current menopause guidance.

If you feel you're being dismissed because of your age

You don't need to walk into the appointment insisting that you have perimenopause. You can ask for the possibility — and other possible explanations — to be properly considered.

You could ask:

“I understand I'm younger than the average age of menopause. Given my symptoms and cycle changes, could perimenopause or early ovarian insufficiency still be something we should consider?”

“If you don't think this is perimenopause, what else could explain these changes?”

“Given my age and symptoms, would any testing or follow-up be appropriate?”

The goal isn't to prove it's perimenopause

It's understandable to want a name for symptoms that are disrupting your life.

But the goal of an evaluation shouldn't be to prove that every change is hormonal. It should be to understand what's happening, consider reasonable explanations and decide whether anything needs investigation or treatment.

Age is useful information in that process. It shouldn't be the entire process.
